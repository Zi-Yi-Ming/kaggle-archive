### From AlexNet to DINOv3: A Practical Backbone History for the RSNA Knee MRI 2026 Challenge

- When you open the attached PDF, you can access the embedded links. -

Author: Sean Lee, Graph‑Axis‑AI Grounded.
Prepared: 2026‑09‑08

Introduction

I have been reviewing the model families that are currently relevant to the RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection
2026 competition, not only as a list of backbones, but as an architectural history: who developed each family,
what problem it was designed to solve, what changed from the previous generation, and whether that
change is actually useful for knee MRI.
The competition is a particularly interesting test bed for this question. The official task is to predict 12 knee
abnormalities at the study level from MRI studies consisting of multiple DICOM series. The dataset is interna‑
tional, multi‑site, multi‑scanner and multi‑protocol, and the training data also includes multilingual radiology
reports. RSNA describes the challenge as its first knee MRI AI challenge and its first challenge to combine medical
images with multilingual report text.
• Kaggle competition: Source
• Kaggle dataset description: Source
• RSNA challenge page: Source
• RSNA 2026 announcement: Source
My current conclusion is that the question “Which backbone is best?” is too narrow.
For this competition, the final performance is more likely to come from the interaction of:
DICOM physical normalization

- series/plane selection
- slice sampling
- study‑level aggregation
- report‑derived supervision
- target‑specific pooling/heads
- an efficient backbone
rather than from simply replacing one backbone with a larger one.
My current primary candidate remains DINOv2‑Small, with ResNet34 as a control model, while newer or
alternative families such as DINOv3, ConvNeXt, EfficientNetV2, ResNeXt, Swin Transformer, MAE and
RadImageNet‑pretrained CNNs are worth controlled ablation rather than automatic promotion to the main
model.

02. CNN Lineage: From AlexNet to ConvNeXt
    The modern CNN era was accelerated by AlexNet, which demonstrated that a deep convolutional network
    trained with GPUs could dramatically improve large‑scale image recognition.
    VGG then showed that a very regular architecture built from repeated 3×3 convolutions could scale effectively
    with depth.
    • VGG: Source
    Google’s Inception / GoogLeNet took another direction: instead of using a single filter scale, it processed fea‑
    tures through multiple branches and scales in parallel.
    • GoogLeNet / Inception: Source
    The major next step was ResNet. Its residual formulation,
    2
    y = F(x) + x
    made much deeper networks substantially easier to optimize.
    • ResNet: Source
    For the current competition, ResNet34 is especially useful as a control model. It is simple, stable, memory‑
    efficient and easy to adapt to 2.5D slice stacks. It also gives us a clean way to determine whether an improvement
    came from the backbone itself or from preprocessing, labels, sampling or aggregation.
    DenseNet introduced dense connectivity, where each layer receives features from all preceding layers. This
    improves feature reuse and gradient flow, but the concatenation pattern can increase activation‑memory traffic.
    • DenseNet: Source
    ResNeXt extended the residual idea by introducing cardinality: multiple parallel transformations with the same
    topology, efficiently implemented with grouped convolutions.
    • ResNeXt: Source
    EfficientNet then focused on coordinated scaling of network depth, width and input resolution instead of scal‑
    ing them independently.
    • EfficientNet: Source
    The later EfficientNetV2 incorporated training speed directly into the design objective and introduced Fused‑
    MBConv blocks.
    • EfficientNetV2: Source
    RegNet is also interesting for Kaggle because it came from studying the design space of networks rather than
    searching for one isolated architecture. FAIR reported that some RegNet variants could outperform EfficientNet
    under comparable FLOPs while running substantially faster on GPUs.
    • RegNet: Source
    Finally, ConvNeXt asked a very useful question: if we modernize ResNet using lessons learned during the Trans‑
    former era, how far can a pure CNN still go?
    • ConvNeXt: Source
    For this challenge, I consider ConvNeXt‑Tiny one of the most useful additional CNN controls because it helps
    separate the question of Transformer superiority from the effect of modern training and architectural design.
03. Transformer and Self‑Supervised Lineage: ViT ‑> MAE / DINO ‑> DINOv2
    ‑\> DINOv3
    The Vision Transformer (ViT) replaced convolutional feature extraction with a sequence of image patches pro‑
    cessed by a Transformer.
    • ViT: Source
    DeiT then showed that ViTs could be trained much more data‑efficiently than the original large‑scale ViT recipe
    suggested.
    • DeiT: Source
    BEiT adapted BERT‑style masked modeling to images.
    • BEiT: Source
    MAE simplified this idea further: mask a very large fraction of image patches, encode only the visible subset and
    reconstruct the missing content.
    • MAE: Source
    3
    This is particularly relevant to medical imaging because unlabeled DICOM data is far easier to obtain than expert
    labels. A future medical pipeline could therefore use large‑scale self‑supervised MRI pretraining before task‑
    specific fine‑tuning.
    DINOv2, introduced by Meta AI, is a self‑supervised visual foundation model trained to produce broadly trans‑
    ferable visual representations.
    • DINOv2 paper: Source
    For the current RSNA Knee competition, DINOv2‑Small remains attractive because it offers strong representa‑
    tion quality without the computational cost of much larger variants.
    The direct successor, DINOv3, was released by Meta in 2025. Meta scaled self‑supervised learning much further
    and introduced changes intended to preserve strong dense visual features during large‑scale training.
    • DINOv3 overview: Source
    • DINOv3 research page: Source
    However, newer does not automatically mean better for this competition.
    A fair DINOv2‑S vs DINOv3‑S comparison should keep everything else fixed:
    • identical DICOM preprocessing,
    • identical physical crop,
    • identical slice sampling,
    • identical resolution,
    • identical cross‑series aggregator,
    • identical target heads,
    • identical folds and training budget.
    Only then can we tell whether the representation itself is better for this task.
04. Swin Transformer and the Medical 3D Branch
    Swin Transformer, developed by Microsoft Research, introduced hierarchical representations and shifted‑
    window attention.
    • Swin Transformer: Source
    This matters because medical imaging is not naturally a collection of unrelated 2D photographs. MRI contains
    local structure, global anatomical context, multiple planes and ordered slices.
    The medical‑imaging community extended Transformer ideas into 3D architectures such as UNETR and Swin
    UNETR.
    • UNETR: Source
    • Swin UNETR: Source
    These models are mainly segmentation architectures rather than direct replacements for a study‑level 12‑label
    classifier. Still, their history is relevant because they demonstrate how hierarchical Transformer representations
    can be adapted to volumetric medical data.
05. U‑Net and nnU‑Net: Why Medical Imaging Has Its Own Architectural
    History
    The history of medical imaging cannot be described only through ImageNet backbones.
    U‑Net became one of the foundational architectures of biomedical image segmentation because it combined a
    contracting encoder with an expanding decoder and skip connections.
    4
    • U‑Net: Source
    nnU‑Net took a different step. Its major contribution was not a new convolution block, but a self‑configuring
    pipeline that automatically adapts preprocessing, architecture, training and post‑processing to the dataset.
    • nnU‑Net: Source
    This is conceptually important for Kaggle as well: sometimes the pipeline and configuration strategy matter
    more than inventing a new backbone.
06. RadImageNet: Domain‑Specific Pretraining Deserves a Serious Test
    One of themost relevant alternatives for this challenge is not a new architecture at all, but a differentpretraining
    domain.
    RadImageNet contains about 1.35 million annotated radiologic images across CT, MRI and ultrasound and
    provides pretrained versions of ResNet50, DenseNet121, InceptionV3 and InceptionResNetV2.
    • RadImageNet paper: Source
    • Official repository: Source
    The original RadImageNet study reported improvements over ImageNet pretraining on several small medical
    datasets, including ACL injury MRI and meniscus tear MRI.
    That makes RadImageNet unusually relevant to this particular competition.
    A useful experiment would be:
    ImageNet‑ResNet50 vs RadImageNet‑ResNet50
    under exactly the same knee‑MRI preprocessing, 2.5D input and study‑level aggregation.
    This would test pretraining‑domain transfer rather than architecture alone.
07. MONAI: Not My Choice for This Competition, but Potentially a Future
    Standard Layer for Medical Imaging AI
    I also tested MONAI during my RSNA Knee MRI work.
    For this specific competition, my current assessment is that MONAI is not the best fit for the main Kaggle
    pipeline. The challenge is dominated by a very specific study‑level classification problem under a strict
    notebook runtime budget, with custom DICOM preprocessing, slice selection, multi‑plane aggregation and
    lightweight backbone experimentation. In that setting, a more direct PyTorch pipeline has been easier for me
    to control, profile and optimize.
    This is not a criticism of MONAI itself. MONAI is a PyTorch‑based, open‑source framework designed specifically
    for healthcare imaging, with medical‑image transforms, caching, domain‑specific networks and losses, multi‑
    GPU support, model bundles, labeling tools and deployment components.
    • Project MONAI: Source
    • MONAI GitHub: Source
    • MONAI Tutorials: Source
    • MONAI Documentation: Source
    Project MONAI was initiated by NVIDIA and King’s College London and has since grown into a broader medical‑
    imaging AI ecosystem. Its architecture is much more relevant to long‑term medical AI development than a sin‑
    gle Kaggle competition because it is designed around reusable healthcare‑imaging workflows rather than one
    competition‑specific classifier.
    My current view is therefore:
    5
    MONAI is not the framework I would prioritize for maximizing this particular RSNA Knee Kaggle
    score today, but I believe MONAI ‑ or a MONAI‑like PyTorch‑native medical‑imaging framework
    ‑ has a strong chance of becoming an important standard layer for future MRI and DICOM AI
    training workflows.
    The reasons are structural:
    • medical‑image‑aware preprocessing instead of generic computer‑vision transforms,
    • native handling of 2D, 2.5D and 3D healthcare‑imaging workflows,
    • reusable DICOM/NIfTI‑oriented data pipelines,
    • medical‑specific losses, metrics and transforms,
    • caching and large‑volume data handling,
    • integration with labeling through MONAI Label,
    • packaging and reproducibility through MONAI Bundles,
    • a path from research code toward deployment.
    For future MRI systems, I expect the more important question to become:
    DICOM / MRI acquisition
    v
    medical-image-aware preprocessing
    v
    MONAI or equivalent medical AI framework
    v
    PyTorch backbone / foundation model
    v
    multi-GPU training
    v
    validation / annotation / deployment
    rather than treating every medical imaging project as an isolated collection of custom image‑loading scripts.
    So my position is deliberately split:
    For this Kaggle competition: custom PyTorch pipeline first.
    For future MRI/DICOM training infrastructure: MONAI deserves serious long‑term attention.
    This is an empirical opinion from my current testing, not a claim that MONAI is already the universal standard
    or that it will necessarily outperform custom pipelines on every task.

09. The More Important Question: Is the Backbone Really the Bottleneck?
    This is where I think the competition becomes more interesting.
    A knee MRI study is not a single image. It contains multiple series, different planes, different sequence sensitiv‑
    ities and a variable number of slices.
    So the full prediction problem is closer to:
    DICOM study
    v
    physical orientation / position ordering
    v
    scanner- and spacing-aware normalization
    v
    series / plane selection
    v
    anatomically meaningful slice sampling
    v
    shared visual encoder
    v
    per-series / per-plane pooling
    v
    cross-series aggregation
    v
    12 target-specific heads
    This means that a better backbone can still lose to a weaker backbone if:
    • slices are physically misordered,
    • PixelSpacing is ignored,
    • the crop represents a different physical field of view across scanners,
    • important series are omitted,
    • irrelevant end slices dominate sampling,
    • all 12 abnormalities are forced to use the same pooling strategy,
    7
    • or weak labels from reports are poorly calibrated.
    That is why I currently view this as a study‑construction and supervision problem first, and a backbone‑
    ranking problem second.
10. Debate: Questions I Would Like to Discuss With Other Competitors
    Debate 1 ‑ DINOv2 vs DINOv3
    Does DINOv3 actually improve knee‑MRI transfer under identical preprocessing and compute, or is the differ‑
    ence smaller than the gain from better study construction?
    Debate 2 ‑ Generic SSL vs radiology‑domain pretraining
    Would a smaller RadImageNet‑pretrained CNN beat a much stronger generic visual foundation model when gold
    labels are scarce?
    Debate 3 ‑ 2.5D hierarchical models vs full 3D models
    Is full 3Dmodeling worth thememory and runtime cost, or does carefully designedmulti‑plane 2.5D aggregation
    capture most of the useful anatomical context?
    Debate 4 ‑ Backbone scaling vs image resolution
    If DINOv2‑Small is already representation‑sufficient, is compute better spent on 336/448 resolution and better
    slice coverage than on DINOv2‑Base/Large?
    Debate 5 ‑ One pooling rule or target‑specific pooling?
    ACL tears, meniscus tears and fractures are focal findings, while osteoarthritis and effusion can be spatially dif‑
    fuse. Should all 12 labels really share one aggregation rule?
    Debate 6 ‑ Are we optimizing the wrong layer?
    If report‑derived supervision and DICOM normalization dominate the error budget, then changing the backbone
    may produce much smaller gains than improving labels, physical coordinates and series selection.
    Debate 7 ‑ Competition framework vs long‑term medical‑imaging framework
    MONAI may add abstraction and workflow overhead that is unnecessary for this particular Kaggle classifier, but
    could that same structure become an advantage when moving from competition code to reusable MRI/DICOM
    training, annotation and deployment pipelines?
    I would be interested in controlled ablations from other competitors, especially experiments that change one
    factor at a time while keeping folds, preprocessing and training budget fixed.

    Then separately ablate:

    This should tell us much more than comparing public leaderboard scores from notebooks with completely dif‑
    ferent pipelines.

    Closing Thought
    My working hypothesis is that the strongest solution will not be defined by a single fashionable backbone.
    It will be defined by how well the system preserves the physical and clinical structure of the MRI study, con‑
    verts weak multimodal supervision into reliable targets, and then uses a sufficiently strong but computationally
    efficient encoder.
    For that reason, I would rather compare systems under controlled conditions than rank architectures by gen‑
    eration or publication date.
    Comments, contradictory ablations and alternative interpretations are welcome.
